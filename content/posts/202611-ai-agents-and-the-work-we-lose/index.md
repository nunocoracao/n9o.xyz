> **Working draft, not finished prose.** This is a research-backed outline for Nuno to write. Keep, reject, or rewrite every prompt and claim. The personal examples below are starting points, not finished sentences in Nuno's voice.

## Intro

**Possible opening scene:** An ordinary day, with agents taking care of grocery shopping, email, calendar, and financial admin. The relief is real. Then the same delegation moves into code and other work that can be satisfying in its own right.

**Question to answer:** What changes when the default response to a task is no longer “I’ll do it,” but “I’ll ask an agent”?

**Possible thesis to test, not accept automatically:** The important distinction may not be human work versus AI work. It may be work that drains us and work through which we build judgment, skill, identity, or satisfaction. Delegating both indiscriminately can save time while quietly removing something valuable.

**Write in your own terms:**
- What task first made AI delegation feel like genuine relief?
- What is an example of a task you actually enjoy doing, even if an agent could do it faster?
- Is the worry mainly about losing skill, losing the pleasure of doing it, or losing the chance to choose?

## What is going on

- The interface is shifting from tools that help us perform a step to agents that can receive an outcome and perform a chain of steps. That moves the user's role from doing toward specifying, reviewing, and deciding.
- Avoid treating “AI at work” as one uniform thing. Evidence varies sharply by task, worker experience, and whether AI is inside or outside its capabilities.
- The ILO's 2025 update estimates one in four workers globally are in occupations with some degree of generative-AI exposure, while concluding most jobs are more likely to be transformed than made redundant. Exposure is not a forecast of job loss. [ILO, 2025](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-2025-update)
- A useful frame is the “jagged frontier”: AI can improve performance on some seemingly similar knowledge tasks and worsen it on others. In a preregistered experiment with 758 consultants, GPT-4 users completed 12.2% more tasks 25.1% faster on tasks inside the frontier, but were 19% less likely to solve a deliberately out-of-frontier task correctly. [Dell’Acqua et al., HBS Working Paper 24-013](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=64700)
- **Author prompt:** Is the agent changing the task itself, or changing who does the task and who remains responsible for the result?

## What we get

### Less friction and more capacity

- In an experiment on professional writing, 453 college-educated professionals completed occupation-specific tasks. ChatGPT reduced average completion time by 40% and increased rated output quality by 18%. This is evidence for those writing tasks, not a general productivity multiplier. [Noy and Zhang, *Science*, 2023](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adh2586)
- In a workplace study of 5,179 customer-support agents, access to a conversational AI assistant increased issues resolved per hour by 14% on average. Benefits were concentrated among novice and lower-skilled workers; effects were minimal for experienced and highly skilled workers. [Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond, NBER Working Paper 31161, later published in *QJE*](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161)
- A 2025 field experiment across 66 firms and 7,137 knowledge workers found that, in the second half of a six-month study, the 80% of treated workers who used an integrated AI tool spent about two fewer hours a week on email and less time working outside regular hours. Researchers did not detect changes in task quantity or composition from individual access alone. [Jaffe et al., “Shifting Work Patterns with Generative AI,” arXiv:2504.11436](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11436)
- These findings can support a concrete point: assistants may reduce repetitive overhead, shorten some tasks, and help less experienced workers access useful patterns.
- **Personal material to write:** groceries, email, calendar, financial organization. Which tasks feel like pure overhead? What did the recovered time make possible, if anything?

### Better leverage, not just faster output

- An agent can make a person more capable of attempting a task, not only faster at a task already mastered. The BCG/Harvard study found sizable gains on tasks within the model’s capabilities. That opens a question about access and agency, not only headcount.
- It can also help with code: exploring an unfamiliar library, prototyping, debugging, or clearing maintenance work. Add a specific example from your own use, including what you still had to understand or review.
- **Author prompt:** Does delegation give you more room to do the work you care about, or does it make even the work you care about feel like a queue to delegate?

## What we lose

### Practice and accumulated judgment

- A task is sometimes valuable partly because doing it is how we learn to do it. Outsourcing the task may also outsource repetitions that build competence. This is a plausible risk, not a proven universal effect of current AI agents.
- A survey of 319 knowledge workers collected 936 examples of workplace GenAI use. Higher confidence in GenAI was associated with less reported critical-thinking effort; higher confidence in one’s own task ability was associated with more. The study is self-reported and observational, so it does not show that AI caused lasting skill loss. It also found that critical thinking shifts toward checking information, integrating the answer, and stewarding the task. [Lee et al., CHI 2025](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3706598.3713778)
- **Author prompt:** What do you notice you can no longer do as fluently when the agent usually does it? Is there a skill you want to keep warm by doing the task yourself sometimes?

### Satisfaction, ownership, and authorship

- Efficiency is not the only reason people do things. Some work is satisfying because it involves making, solving, or exercising taste. An assistant can produce the artifact while removing the process that made the artifact meaningful.
- Keep this as personal testimony, not a claim that research proves AI takes meaning away. Name the actual activities: perhaps coding, writing, researching, or organizing something yourself. Which ones feel fulfilling, and why?
- Ask whether the gain is time *for* something meaningful, or simply a life in which fewer things are done by you. Both may be true at once.
- **Author prompt:** What would feel different about finishing a piece of code you wrote versus approving one an agent wrote? What parts of the process do you want to retain?

### Confidence and the illusion of progress

- In a randomized trial involving 16 experienced open-source developers and 246 real tasks in their own mature projects, developers allowed to use early-2025 AI tools took 19% longer on average. They still estimated afterward that AI had made them 20% faster. The result is narrow: it concerns this small sample, these developers, tasks, and tools at that time. The authors explicitly caution against generalizing it to all developers or work. [Becker et al., METR, 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)
- The contrast is useful for a personal essay: feeling less effortful or seeing code appear quickly is not the same as finishing the whole task sooner or understanding it better.
- **Author prompt:** How do you know an agent saved time after including the time spent prompting, checking, correcting, and maintaining the result?

### Responsibility does not disappear

- Delegating execution does not delegate accountability. Someone still defines the goal, checks the result, and owns the consequences. In the jagged-frontier experiment, one out-of-frontier task showed how AI assistance could lower correctness.
- Consider whether “having an agent do it” creates a new kind of work: reviewing, coordinating, and deciding what deserves human attention. This could be a useful bridge between the productivity story and the personal one.
- **Author prompt:** Which decisions should remain yours even when the agent can execute them?

## Conclusion

**Do not force a simple verdict.** The evidence supports real gains in some settings and meaningful uncertainty in others. The personal question can remain unresolved.

**Possible ending direction:** The goal may not be to preserve every task, nor to automate every task. It may be to decide which kinds of effort are friction to remove and which kinds of effort are part of what makes the work yours.

**Write your own answer to these:**
- What will you gladly delegate?
- What do you want to keep doing, even if it is slower?
- What would you like to learn or remain capable of doing without an agent?
- What is one boundary you might experiment with, such as “agent handles the inbox, I still write the first version of an essay”?
- Is the right measure productivity, time recovered, quality, learning, satisfaction, or some combination?

## Research notes and references

Use these as a source file, not as a mandate to cite everything in the finished essay. Prefer the primary paper or institution link. Distinguish experimental results from surveys, working papers, and your own interpretation.

1. Noy, Shakked, and Whitney Zhang. “Experimental Evidence on the Productivity Effects of Generative Artificial Intelligence.” *Science* 381, no. 6654 (2023). [Article](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adh2586). Experiment: professional writing tasks; 453 participants; large task-specific time and quality effects.
2. Dell’Acqua, Fabrizio, et al. “Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier: Field Experimental Evidence of the Effects of Artificial Intelligence on Knowledge Worker Productivity and Quality.” Harvard Business School Working Paper 24-013 (2023). [Paper record and abstract](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=64700). Experiment with 758 consultants; results differed for in-frontier and out-of-frontier tasks.
3. Brynjolfsson, Erik, Danielle Li, and Lindsey R. Raymond. “Generative AI at Work.” NBER Working Paper 31161 (2023; published in *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2025). [NBER record](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161). Workplace data from 5,179 customer-support agents.
4. Jaffe, Sonia, et al. “Shifting Work Patterns with Generative AI.” arXiv:2504.11436 (2025). [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11436). Field experiment across 66 firms and 7,137 knowledge workers; time use, not direct evidence of skill loss.
5. Lee, Hao-Ping, et al. “The Impact of Generative AI on Critical Thinking: Self-Reported Reductions in Cognitive Effort and Confidence Effects From a Survey of Knowledge Workers.” *CHI 2025*. [ACM DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778). Survey and qualitative examples; associative/self-reported evidence, not a causal test of long-term deskilling.
6. Becker, Joel, et al. “Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity.” METR (2025). [Study and limitations](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/). Small randomized trial in experienced developers’ own repositories; useful counterexample, not a universal result.
7. Gmyrek, Paweł, Janine Berg, and David Bescond. “Generative AI and Jobs: A 2025 Update.” International Labour Organization (20 May 2025). [Research brief](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-2025-update). Occupational exposure analysis; exposure is not equivalent to job displacement.
8. Deci, Edward L., and Richard M. Ryan. “Self-Determination Theory.” For a high-level entry point on autonomy, competence, and motivation at work, see [Ryan and Deci, *Nature Reviews Psychology* (2022)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44159-022-00056-w). This provides a lens for the personal argument, not direct evidence about AI delegation.

### Evidence limits to preserve

- Do not treat task exposure as a prediction that a whole job will disappear.
- Do not generalize one task study to all professional work.
- The critical-thinking study is self-reported and does not establish permanent cognitive decline.
- The METR coding result is a small, specific trial using tools available in early 2025.
- Claims about fulfillment, identity, and what the author wants to keep doing should come from Nuno’s own experience.
